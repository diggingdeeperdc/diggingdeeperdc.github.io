Let's Talk About Peace

2026 · 12 min read

I fully expect that either I will get smashed for what I am sharing, or maybe nobody sees it at all — and that's okay, because this is just what is on my heart to share.

I have heard it taught that Jesus is the Prince of Peace, and by extension taught not to be argumentative — that wherever you go you are to bring a spirit of peace with you, and that as a Christian you should always be nice. Even if it is not expressed exactly in those words, it is often, or usually, implied in that way.

It is often taught that Christians should not have a harsh word to say, and that they should conduct themselves in some sort of monastic manner, or have an almost stoic approach to life where nothing phases them — and that expressions or feelings of anger and frustration are generally unacceptable.

This is mainly because as Christians we know Jesus as the Prince of Peace, as it is one of his names in Scripture. Another verse sympathetic to this kind of teaching is Matthew 5:9 — God blesses those who work for peace, for they will be called the children of God.

Generally speaking, I think when we read the Bible and go deeper on this topic, we can come to a better understanding of who Jesus is based on what the Scriptures tell us — and we may come to a different conclusion about what our Christian walk should look like. I am hoping to change our thinking away from the idea that as Christians we should prioritise our peace, and the peace of others we come in contact with, first and foremost, in order to win people to Christ.

Jesus said in Matthew 10:34–36:

Do not think that I have come to bring peace to the earth. I have not come to bring peace, but a sword (machaira, a Greek short sword). For I have come to set a man against his father, and a daughter against her mother, and a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law. And a person's enemies will be those of his own household.

Matthew 10:34–36

The sword mentioned here speaks of division — the fact that a single person coming to Christ in a family will cause division.

Jesus said in Luke 12:49:

I have come to set the world on fire, and I wish it were already burning! I have a terrible baptism of suffering ahead of me, and I am under a heavy burden until it is accomplished. Do you think I have come to bring peace to the earth? No, I have come to divide people against each other! From now on families will be split apart, three in favour of me and two against — or two in favour and three against.

Luke 12:49–52

Before the Last Supper, Jesus tells the disciples to purchase two swords, and also reprimands Peter for cutting off the slave's ear — probably with one of the very swords they spoke about. This must have been confusing for Peter. Nonetheless, Jesus was going to the cross, and he knew life for his disciples was going to change and they were going to need to arm themselves. The environment they were living in was going to be far more hostile toward them after he was sacrificed on the cross. In fact, they would be better off owning a sword and being safe than owning a cloak to keep themselves warm — which is why Jesus tells them to sell their cloaks to buy swords.

Jesus willingly died on the cross as a sacrifice for our sins — a perfect sacrifice, done once and for all, that does not have to be repeated again. This is a marvellous truth, one I am truly grateful for. Jesus did this of his own will, when he could have commanded legions of angels to come down from heaven and save him. Many Christians point to this and exclaim, "See, Jesus did this for peace — see how peaceful he is!" I think this is misleading. Jesus himself stated he did not come to bring peace. He came to be the perfect sacrifice — and that is an entirely different objective than being a bringer of, or an example of, peace.

So what peace does Jesus bring?

Jesus gives us peace of mind and heart. In John 14:27 he says to his disciples:

I am leaving you with a gift — peace of mind and heart. And the peace I give is a gift the world cannot give.

John 14:27

Jesus was wise, and at times answered the Pharisees indirectly through parables and stories — however, Jesus did not shy away from confrontation.

So the Jewish leaders began harassing Jesus for breaking the Sabbath rules. But Jesus replied, "My Father is always working, and so am I." So the Jewish leaders tried all the harder to find a way to kill him. For he not only broke the Sabbath, he called God his Father, thereby making himself equal with God.

John 5:16–18

Jesus does not stop there — he continues, absolutely giving it to the Pharisees about how wrong they are, for the rest of chapter 5. He tells them how the Scriptures they worship will not save them, and that Moses himself will accuse them before God at judgement, because they don't believe in Jesus.

Jesus has a sword. In Revelation 1:16 it is described as coming out of his mouth, and that it is a sharp, two-edged sword. In fact Jesus tells John that he will use his sword. This is what he says to the church of Pergamum in Revelation 2:16 — Repent of your sin, or I will come to you suddenly and fight against them with the sword of my mouth.

And you may be thinking, well, that can't be too bad — I mean, at least he isn't swinging a real sword with his hands, that would be much scarier. Jesus doesn't condone violence, he wouldn't hurt anyone! Well, I have to tell you that actually the sword that comes out of Jesus' mouth is scary. John tells us exactly what happens when Jesus uses the sword of his mouth, in chapter 19:15 —

Coming out of his mouth is a sharp sword with which to strike down the nations. "He will rule them with an iron sceptre." He treads the winepress of the fury of the wrath of God Almighty.

Revelation 19:15

It is amazing that the same Jesus who will wield such devastating power and destruction upon evil is the one who gives peace of mind and heart. Judgement is a real thing for those who do not believe in Jesus Christ as their Lord and Saviour. One day Jesus will come back, and he will wield his sword.

A peaceable image, half the picture

A common theme in a lot of modern Christian churches is the idea that someone who is successful in faith — as well as in life — does not argue, is agreeable, and carries themselves with such peace that the world can see how gracious they are, and be converted to Christianity by it. And perhaps for a minority of people, that is true, and that works.

It is also true that compared to other countries, Christians in Australia are much less likely to experience persecution for what they believe. Many Christians in countries considered less developed than their Western counterparts are martyred because of their faith.

I believe the reason Christians do not experience persecution in Australia is because they are not vocal about their faith, and even attempt to hide it from others. I have been there myself, particularly in the workplace — what I have experienced is that, slowly over time, I am changed by the environment I am in, instead of having any meaningful impact on the ethics or morals of the organisation.

Perhaps the reason Christians don't suffer persecution in Australia is because we are not vocal, and as a collective we do not stand up for our principles or express what we believe to others. What would happen if a Christian employee said outright that they do not agree with LGBTQ+ propaganda, and pushed back — because they want to be nice, agreeable, and bring a sweet spirit wherever they go? They may even view their own passive behaviour as a way to turn the other cheek and show love by not causing a disagreement.

I am not writing this to condemn anybody. I have been in situations where I wanted to push back but did not. I was on campus learning, and I was simply told donuts were available. I was interested. It was only once I got to the table where all the donuts were that the lady explained the reason they were handing out donuts was to show people who identify as LGBTQ+ that we emotionally support them. At that moment I wanted to push back, and I no longer wanted the donut — but instead of causing a scene in a crowded area, and perhaps facing ostracism from those I learn with, I took the donut and I ate it. On one hand, it is a meaningless, cheap token gesture, as donuts do not in any way tangibly give anybody any support. I could have pointed this out, and also that this table actually makes me feel like what I believe in is not supported. But I did not. I should not have taken the donut at the least, and at the most I should have voiced my concerns, if not publicly then privately.

How many Christians are willing to loudly and proudly declare their beliefs, and also demonstrate them for all to see?

God blesses those who are persecuted for doing right, for the Kingdom of Heaven is theirs.

Matthew 5:10

Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied.

Matthew 5:6

We are called to be different. We are called to stand up to, and fight against, the things that are wrong. We are not called to be passive in these situations. Jesus told us in John 15:18:

If the world hates you, remember it hated me first. The world would love you as one of its own if you belonged to it, but you are no longer part of the world. I chose you to come out of the world, so it hates you.

John 15:18–19

And Paul says to the Corinthian church:

We are human, but we don't wage war as humans do. We use God's mighty weapons, not worldly weapons, to knock down the strongholds of human reasoning and to destroy false arguments. We destroy every proud obstacle that keeps people from knowing God. We capture their rebellious thoughts and teach them to obey Christ.

2 Corinthians 10:3–5

We are supposed to be destroying false arguments — destroying every proud obstacle that keeps people from knowing God.

If we do that, we will likely be persecuted for it. Jesus has this to say about it:

If any of you wants to be my follower, you must give up your own way, take up your cross, and follow me. If you try to hang on to your life, you will lose it. But if you give up your life for my sake, you will save it. And what do you benefit if you gain the whole world but lose your own soul? Is anything worth more than your soul?

Matthew 16:24–26

Don't copy the behaviour and customs of this world, but let God transform you into a new person by changing the way you think. Then you will learn to know God's will for you, which is good and pleasing and perfect.

Romans 12:2

Salt and light

We are called to be salt and light.

You are the salt of the earth. But what good is salt if it has lost its flavour? Can you make it salty again? It will be thrown out and trampled underfoot as worthless.

You are the light of the world — like a city on a hilltop that cannot be hidden. No one lights a lamp and then puts it under a basket. Instead, a lamp is placed on a stand, where it gives light to everyone in the house. In the same way, let your good deeds shine out for all to see, so that everyone will praise your heavenly Father.

Matthew 5:13–16

Salt preserves. It preserves the good things in a culture; it preserves life and protects against spoiling and rottenness. It enhances flavour. We should be salt to everything and everyone around us, wherever we go.

Light shines into dark places and exposes evil deeds. Jesus tells his followers to stand in his light as long as he is around, so that they may become children of the light. As Christians we should be like a city on a hill. What we believe should shine so brightly that we can be seen from miles and miles away. People will look to us to know which direction to head in.

Sometimes our good deeds may cause conflict. Those good deeds may lead to persecution. Or, as Jesus puts it:

I have told you these things, so that in me you may have peace. In this world you will have trouble. But take heart! I have overcome the world.

John 16:33
