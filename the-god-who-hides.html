The God Who Hides

March 2026 · 6 min read

There is a strange line tucked into the middle of Isaiah, easy to read past, that has never quite let me go. In the space of a single verse the prophet turns from the nations to God himself and says something almost accusing: that this is a God who hides.

Truly, you are a God who hides himself, O God of Israel, the Saviour.

Isaiah 45:15

It is a verse that does not try to comfort us before it is honest with us. Hiddenness is set right beside salvation, as if the two belong together rather than fighting each other. And anyone who has prayed for long enough knows the experience it names — the season when heaven seems to have gone quiet, when the practices that once carried you feel like going through motions, when you are not doubting God so much as unable to find him.

The silence is not the same as absence

We tend to read God's silence as a verdict. If I cannot feel him, he must have withdrawn; if he has withdrawn, it must be something I have done. But the whole witness of Scripture presses back on that instinct. The same psalms that cry out "why have you forsaken me" are prayed, and a prayer is not shouted into an empty room. Lament is itself a form of faith — it is what trust sounds like when it is in pain.

There is an older idea here that we have mostly lost. The writers of the Christian tradition spoke of a hiddenness that is not punishment but formation. A father who catches every stumble teaches his child nothing about walking. Love that never lets you out of its sight is not love but surveillance. Perhaps some of what we experience as God's distance is in fact the room he makes for us to grow up.

What the hiding protects

Consider what would happen if God were always vividly, unmistakably present. We would follow him, certainly — the way you obey a boss who is standing over your shoulder. But we would never learn to love him for himself rather than for the felt reward of his nearness. The hiding, uncomfortable as it is, guards something precious: the possibility of a love that is freely given, in the dark, when there is nothing in it for us but him.

This does not make the dark seasons pleasant. It is not meant to. But it changes what they mean. The absence you are feeling may not be abandonment at all. It may be the particular shape love takes when it is teaching you to walk.

And there is one more thing worth saying. The verse in Isaiah does not end with the hiding. It ends with a name: the Saviour. The God who hides is, in the same breath, the God who saves. The hiddenness is real, but it is not the last word — and it was never the whole story.
